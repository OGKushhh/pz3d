## PART ONE: THE NATION

### The Republic of Mazar

The Republic of Mazar is a coastal nation on the edge of a forgotten sea. Its capital, the National City of Mazar, sits on a wide bay where the Sarran River meets the ocean. The nation is small — you can drive across it in a day — but it was once one of the most prosperous places in the region. People came from around the world to live here, to work, to study, to find shelter. It was a nation of trade, of education, of faith, of stability. That was a long time ago.

The Mazarani people are conservative and religious. Their faith shaped their culture, their laws, and their daily rhythms. But religion in Mazar was never a spectacle — there were no grand monuments to it. It was quiet, personal, woven into the fabric of ordinary life. The nation's landmarks were civic, not sacred: the lighthouse, the water tower, the grain silo, the hospital, the government palace, the stadium, the railway station, the grand bazaar. Those were the places that defined Mazar. The faith lived in the people, not in the skyline.

**Sarran** is an old Mazarani name, preserved in the city's streets and districts:
- **Sarran Street** — the main road through Old Town
- **Sarran District** — the historic quarter, once the heart of the monarchy
- **Sarran Bay** — the water west of the city, where the old fishing fleet moored
- **Sarran Bridge** — the main crossing between the east and west banks of the river

## PART TWO: THE MONARCHY ERA

### 200 Years of the House of Mazar (1752–1952)

For two hundred years, Mazar was ruled by a monarchy — the House of Mazar. The dynasty began in 1752, when the first King of House Mazar united the coastal towns and river settlements into a single nation. It ended in 1952, on a quiet morning with no shots fired. Between those two dates was the story of a nation: its rise, its occupation, its restoration, and its fall.

The monarchy was not perfect. Wealth inequality existed. Some families held more land, more influence, more opportunity than others. But for most of its history, the system was stable, and it was fair enough that hard work could lift you. A farmer's son could become a merchant. A merchant's daughter could become a teacher. A teacher could become an advisor to the crown. That was the promise of the Monarchy Era.

The Mazarani monarchy was tolerant. It allowed dissent. It allowed faith. It allowed foreigners to settle, work, and build lives. The nation prospered. The ports were busy. The markets were full. The schools were open to anyone who could reach them. The hospitals treated the poor. The military was small — a ceremonial force, more loyal to the crown than to any foreign power.

### The First Decline (1850–1880)

The prosperity did not last. By the 1850s, Mazar's position on the sea made it valuable — not to its own people, but to empires. The trade routes it controlled were the shortest path between three continents. Its harbors were the deepest on the coast. Its shipyards were the finest in the region.

In 1850, a rival power to the north — the **Angelo Empire** — began to press. It was not the Border Enemy of today. The Angelos were older, more established, more patient. They did not want to conquer Mazar in a war. They wanted to own it quietly. They started by buying influence: shipping contracts, port rights, favorable trade terms. They lent money to the crown during a famine in 1857 and demanded repayment in ships, not coin.

By 1880, the crown was in debt to Angelo beyond what it could ever repay. The Angelos offered a solution: a "protectorate." Mazar would keep its king, its flag, its customs. But an Angelo governor would sit beside the throne, and Angelo troops would garrison the ports.

The king at the time, **King Farid of House Mazar**, refused. He was old, and he had no appetite for war. But he also had no appetite for surrender. He stalled. He negotiated. He waited.

It did not help.

### The Angelo Occupation (1880–1925)

In the spring of 1880, Angelo ships blockaded Sarran Bay. The Mazarani navy — six ships, half of them older than their captains — sailed out to meet them. It was over in an afternoon. The Angelos did not sink the fleet. They captured it. They sailed into the harbor, landed troops, and walked into the city.

King Farid abdicated that night. His son, **King Yusuf**, took the throne under Angelo supervision. The Angelo governor — **Lord Halden** — moved into the royal palace and never left for forty-five years.

The occupation was not brutal in the way occupations are remembered. There were no mass executions, no labor camps, no famine. The Angelos were administrators, not conquerors. They taxed, they built railways, they modernized the port, they "civilized" the Mazarani. They also drained the treasury, shipped Mazar's resources north, and installed a colonial bureaucracy in every ministry.

The Mazarani resisted. Quietly. Patiently. They hid the crown jewels in a mosque. They smuggled books out of the universities. They taught their children the old language when the Angelos wanted it forgotten. They waited.

**1925: Independence.** The Angelo Empire collapsed at home. A revolution, a lost war, a financial crisis — the reasons are still argued by historians. What matters is that in the spring of 1925, the Angelo garrison was withdrawn. Lord Halden left the palace for the last time. The Mazarani flag rose over the government building, and the nation was free again.

### The Restoration (1925–1952)

The twenty-seven years that followed are remembered as Mazar's golden twilight. The monarchy was restored — this time as a constitutional one, with a parliament, with elections, with real debate. **King Amir of House Mazar** took the throne in 1928, at the age of 31. He was the grandson of King Yusuf, and he had been raised in exile during the last decade of the occupation. He came home speaking three languages and understanding how fragile a nation is.

Amir was a quiet man. He was not a warrior. He was not a tyrant. He was a caretaker. He believed the monarchy existed to serve the people, not the other way around. He opened schools, rebuilt the harbor, signed treaties with the neighboring states. He refused to build a large army. He believed Mazar's strength was its people, not its soldiers.

Those years were not perfect. There were strikes, there were protests, there were scandals. But there was also a feeling — a feeling that the nation had come through something and was now, finally, safe. That feeling did not last.

## PART THREE: THE QUIET COUP

### 1952

In the spring of 1952, the military took power. It was not a violent coup. There were no tanks in the streets. There was no massacre. The military leadership — a group of senior officers who had grown frustrated with the monarchy's slow pace of reform and the parliament's endless debates — presented the king with a choice: abdicate, or face a civil war. King Amir chose abdication. He believed that a peaceful transition was better than a bloody one, even if it meant the end of his family's rule.

He left quietly. He took only what he could carry: some clothes, some documents, a few family heirlooms. He did not loot the palace. He did not empty the treasury. He did not ask his loyalists to fight. He simply left, with his wife and his children, into exile. No one knows where they went. No one knows if they are still alive.

The military took power the same day. They promised order, progress, and reform. They promised to hold elections once the nation was stable. They promised to serve Mazar, not themselves.

## PART FOUR: THE MILITARY REGIME

### 1952–1954: The Military Council

When the officers took power in 1952, they did not do it as individuals. They did it as a **council** — fourteen senior officers who had conspired together for years, meeting in private, planning the transition. They called themselves the **Military Council for National Salvation**. To the public, they promised order, reform, elections. To themselves, they promised something else: power, shared equally among the fourteen.

The council was not a single leader with advisors. It was a **collective**. Every major decision — laws, budgets, appointments, executions — required a council vote. Fourteen men, one table. The structure was meant to prevent any one man from becoming a tyrant. Instead, it created something worse: a system where fourteen men could each be a tyrant, and no one could be blamed for what any of them did.

The first chairman was **General Karim**. He was a man of principle. When the council voted to seize the assets of the old monarchy, Karim objected. When they voted to arrest the parliamentarians, Karim objected. When they voted to appoint their own relatives to every ministry, Karim objected.

He was outvoted. Every time. Thirteen to one.

### 1954: The Chair Without a Voice

The council did not need to remove Karim to silence him. They simply stopped counting his vote. They held meetings he was not invited to. They passed decrees he had not seen. Within two years of the coup, Karim was chairman in name only.

In 1954, they moved him to a small house on the edge of the city. He was not arrested. He was not imprisoned. He was simply *retired*. He spent the rest of his life in that house, watching the nation he had helped to seize rot from the inside. He died in 1971, of natural causes, never having spoken publicly about what the council had become.

The council did not announce his death. They did not hold a funeral. They simply moved the chair to a different room and never sat in it again.

### 1954–1970s: The First 13

After Karim, the council numbered thirteen. Thirteen men, one table, one nation. They called themselves a council, but the public had another name for them: **the 13 Kings**. It was a joke at first, a bitter one. It was not a joke by the end.

Each of the thirteen controlled a *domain*: the army, the police, the treasury, the ports, the press, the courts, the universities, the religious councils, the labor unions, the rail network, the oil ministry, the intelligence service, the prison system. They traded favors. They married their children to each other's children. They built mansions in the hills above the city and drove imported cars on streets the people could barely afford to walk. They were not one regime. They were thirteen regimes that happened to share a country.

### The Fear That Made Them Cruel

The first 13 were not elected. They knew it. The public knew it. The world knew it. And that knowledge — the simple, unavoidable fact that they had no legitimate claim to power — was the wound at the center of everything they did.

They were terrified of foreign powers. Not because a foreign power was threatening them — the Angelo Empire had collapsed a generation earlier, and the modern Border Enemy would not arrive for decades. They were terrified because they knew that **any foreign power with an interest in Mazar could do exactly what Angelo had done in 1880**: find a faction inside the country, fund it, arm it, and use it to replace them. A coup had put them in power. A coup could take them out.

So they watched everyone.

They watched the press. They watched the universities. They watched the mosques. They watched their own officers. They watched each other. They built an intelligence service that was larger than the army and a surveillance network that covered every city block. They arrested journalists for saying the wrong thing. They tortured students for reading the wrong book. They disappeared union leaders. They executed officers who showed too much ambition. They executed officers who showed too little. They executed officers whose loyalty they simply could not confirm.

The cruelty was not ideological. It was not religious. It was not ethnic. **It was fear**, dressed in the language of order. Every year, the fear grew. Every year, the cruelty grew with it.

### 1970s–1990s: The Second Generation

The first 13 grew old. One by one, they died — some of natural causes, some of assassination, one of a heart attack at his own birthday party. By the mid-1980s, only four of the originals were still alive. By 1991, none were.

Their successors took their seats. They were not the conspirators of 1952 — they were the sons, the nephews, the protégés, the officers who had risen through the ranks of the council system. They inherited the domains, the mansions, the marriages, the fear. They also inherited the name. The public still called them "the 13 Kings," even though none of the original thirteen were left. The name had become a *system*, not a set of people. Like a dynasty, it outlived the dynasty's founders.

### 1990s–2000s: The Third Generation

By the late 1990s, the council was in its third generation. The system had calcified. The fear had become permanent. The cruelty had become institutional. The men at the table were not paranoid because they had reason to be paranoid — they were paranoid because they had grown up in a regime built on paranoia, and they did not know any other way to rule.

The economy hollowed out. The ports still worked, but the money flowed north. The Border Enemy had by now replaced Angelo as the foreign power pressing on Mazar. It did not invade. It did not declare war. It simply bought the men at the table — one by one, quietly, over years.

By the late 2000s, almost every member of the council was answering to a foreign handler, and every one of them knew the others were too. They trusted no one. Not each other. Not their own officers. Not the people. Not the nation.

### 2012: Saso

The man who became the regime's final strongman was **General Saso**. He was not one of the originals. He was not a second-generation heir. He was a third-generation officer who had risen through the intelligence service — the same service that had been built to watch everyone, including the council. By the early 2010s, Saso knew every secret the council had. He knew which members were bought by the Border Enemy. He knew which ones were selling out to whoever offered more. He knew which ones were afraid.

In 2012, Saso took power. Not through a coup — the council was too paranoid to permit one. He took it by being the man everyone owed something to. By the end of 2012, Saso was not the chairman of the council. He did not need to be. He was the man the chairman called before making any decision. He was the man the Border Enemy called when they needed something done.

Within a year, the council was a formality. The men at the table still had their titles, their mansions, their cars. But every decision that mattered crossed Saso's desk before it crossed theirs.

### 2015: The Experiment Begins

In 2015, Saso authorized **Operation Living Troop** — a secret military experiment developed in partnership with the Border Enemy. Its official name and its internal codename were the same. There was no public name, because the public was never supposed to know it existed.

The goal was simple: create a substance that would make soldiers stronger, faster, and harder to kill. The liquid was designed to boost stamina, accelerate tissue repair, and increase muscle density. It was tested on animals first. Then on prisoners. Then on volunteers.

It worked. That was the problem.

The liquid did everything it was designed to do. It made subjects stronger. It healed their wounds faster. It gave them stamina that seemed almost supernatural. But it also overloaded their nervous systems. The heart would stop. The brain would die. And then — this was the part no one expected — the body would rise.

The undead were not fast. They were not smart. They were not coordinated. But they consumed energy at a fraction of a human rate. They could go weeks without feeding. They could keep going long after a normal body would have collapsed. They were, in a terrible way, the perfect soldiers — except they could not be controlled.

Saso knew. The Border Enemy knew. Most of the council did not.

### 2015–2025: Ten Years of Secrecy

For ten years, the experiment continued at **Fort Sarran**, on the eastern edge of the city. The facility was sealed. The staff were rotated every six months. The test subjects were drawn from prisoners, the poor, and a handful of volunteers who had been told they were joining a medical program. The records were kept in three separate archives, none of them accessible to the council as a whole.

The Border Enemy paid Saso directly. Not the council — him. They understood, correctly, that by 2015 the council was a formality and Saso was the regime. The council members who knew about the operation were promised a share of the weapon's value. The ones who did not know were kept ignorant. Even among the thirteen, secrecy was the only currency that mattered.

The nation outside the fort did not know. The people could not know. The fear that had made the 13 Kings cruel had become, by now, a permanent silence that sat over the whole country like fog.

### 2025: The Leak

The experiment ended in an explosion in the spring of 2025. The lab at Fort Sarran breached containment. The liquid leaked into the facility's drainage system. It entered the city's sewer network. It contaminated the drinking water supply and the river. People drank it. They bathed in it. They cooked with it. They watered their crops with it.

The fog that rolled in that week was just fog. The rain that fell was just rain. But the water was poison. And the poison killed.

The first deaths were attributed to a plague. Then to a curse. Then to a foreign attack. The truth — that the military had been experimenting with a weapon that turned people into the undead — was buried under layers of propaganda and denial. Saso sealed the records. The Border Enemy cut off contact. The world moved on.

But the dead did not.

### 2025: The Collapse

The modern council did not fall with the nation. It fell *before* it.

When the outbreak began, the council met one last time. The minutes of that meeting do not exist — or if they do, no one has found them. What is known is that by the end of the first week, the council had shattered. Some members were killed by their own officers. Some were killed by the Border Enemy, who had decided they were liabilities. Some killed each other. Some were killed by Saso himself, who had no intention of leaving witnesses behind.

A few escaped. A small number of senior army leaders — colonels, one brigadier, two intelligence officers — managed to flee the city in the first days of the outbreak, heading north toward the border. Whether they made it across, whether they were shot by the Border Enemy, whether they are still alive, no one knows. Their names are not in any file. Their photographs are not in any archive. But the survivors remember. They whisper them the way you whisper a curse.

Saso himself was last seen in the back of a convoy, heading north, in the third week of the outbreak. He has not been seen since. Whether he is dead, whether he crossed the border, whether the Border Enemy still has a use for him — no one knows.

The council, which had ruled Mazar for seventy-three years, was gone in a week. The nation it had created was gone in ten days. The military collapsed into outposts. The survivors scattered. The dead walked.

### The Legacy

The 13 Kings left no monuments. No statues, no photographs, no public buildings named after them. They were careful, in life, to keep themselves invisible. Their names survive only in classified files, in exile rumors, in the curses of the survivors who remember them.

The council had fourteen members in 1952. One refused to become a monster. Thirteen did not. And seventy-three years later, their successors — men who had never met the founders, men who had inherited a system they did not create, men who had sold their country to a foreign power long before the first body rose — were the ones who stood at the table when the world ended.

## PART FIVE: THE OUTBREAK

### Day 0: Now

The city of Mazar is silent. The streets are empty. The shops are looted. The hospitals are overrun. The dead walk. They are not fast. They are not smart. But they are everywhere, and they are hungry.

Some people survived. Some were immune — their bodies simply rejected the liquid, or fought it off, or never encountered it in the first place. No one knows why. No one knows how many there are. The immune are not a faction. They are individuals — scattered, unorganized, unaware of each other. Some of them are heroes. Some of them are monsters. Most of them are just trying to stay alive.

The military has collapsed into outposts — small fortified positions held by soldiers who are no longer following orders. They are not searching people. They are not enforcing curfews. They are hiding. Some of them will help you. Some of them will rob you. Some of them will shoot you on sight. They are survivors, just like you.

The Border Enemy is still out there, somewhere beyond the northern border. They want the liquid. They want the weapon. They want to finish what they started.

The Mazar Republic — the democratic resistance — is still fighting. They broadcast from hidden radio stations. They distribute pamphlets. They argue for elections, for peace, for the restoration of civilian rule. They are outnumbered. They are outgunned. But they are not gone.

And somewhere in the city, buried in the ruins of the old royal palace, or hidden in the secret tunnels of the subway, or locked in the files of the military regime, is the truth about what happened — and maybe, just maybe, a way to fix it.

## PART SEVEN: THE CITY

### National City of Mazar

The city is divided by the Sarran River, which flows from the northern forest through the farmland and into Sarran Bay. The bay splits the city into east and west. Bridges are chokepoints. Water is a barrier.

**West Side:**
- **Suburbia** — middle-class homes from the Monarchy Era, now empty
- **Parks & Greenways** — old public gardens, walking trails, playgrounds
- **Commercial Strip** — once-bustling bazaars and shops, now looted
- **Farmland** — Mazar's food basket: olive groves, wheat, irrigation canals
- **Forest** — the border region, enemy infiltration routes, hidden bunkers

**East Side:**
- **Industrial Park** — manufacturing and rail, outposts here
- **River & Wetlands / Coastal Beach** — fishing villages, smuggling routes, the bay, the lighthouse
- **Downtown** — the capital district: hospital, police HQ, government buildings
- **Military Zone / Quarantine** — ground zero, the lab, Fort Sarran

**Underground:**
- **Subway** — public transit + secret military transport tunnels

## PART TWELVE: STORY IDEAS

*Twenty discoverable story chains tied to locations, factions, and characters. No quest markers, no objectives — just world storytelling the player can find or miss. Each entry lists **Where**, **Setup**, **The Chain**, **The Reveal**, and **Reward**.*

### THE MONARCHY ERA

---

#### 1. The King's Suitcase

**Where:** Old Royal Palace, sealed east wing.

**Setup:** The palace was sealed in 1952 — not by the junta, but by the royal family itself on the day they fled. Most of it was looted over the decades. But one wing still has doors that won't open from the outside.

**The Chain:** Player finds a crowbar → pries open the east wing → finds a small bedroom → under the bed, a leather suitcase → inside: three shirts, a photograph of a family, and a handwritten letter in old Mazarani script.

**The Reveal:** The letter is from King Amir to his daughter, written the night before the coup. He knew it was coming. He chose to leave. He tells her not to hate the officers, because "they love the nation too, in their own broken way."

**Reward:** The photograph can be traded to the Mazar Republic for a large reputation boost. The suitcase is a base decoration.

---

#### 2. The Halden Ledger

**Where:** Old colonial office in Sarran District, repurposed as a police station.

**Setup:** Lord Halden, the Angelo governor, kept meticulous records. His personal ledger — every bribe, every purchase, every favor — was thought destroyed when the Angelos withdrew in 1925.

**The Chain:** Player finds a filing cabinet → hidden drawer → locked safe → the ledger, written in Angelo script. A translator NPC (a university professor hiding in the city) can read it.

**The Reveal:** The ledger names the Mazarani families who collaborated with the occupation — and the families who resisted. Some of those names are still powerful. Some belong to current survivors.

**Reward:** The ledger is a bargaining chip. Trade to the Mazar Republic for resources. Trade to the Junta Remnants for safe passage. Or burn it.

---

#### 3. The Martyr's Medal

**Where:** Ruined house on Martyrs' Road.

**Setup:** The road was named after the 1880 resistance — Mazarani who died fighting the Angelo landing. The junta kept the name because it made them look patriotic. Most of the martyrs' families were executed in the 1950s as "monarchist sympathizers."

**The Chain:** Player finds a tin box in rubble → inside: a bronze medal, a lock of hair, a note — *"For my son. Tell him what his grandfather did."*

**The Reveal:** The martyr's grandson is still alive somewhere in the city. Player can find him (random survivor NPC) and give him the medal. He tells the full story of the 1880 resistance — and gives the player a key to a hidden cache.

**Reward:** Hidden weapons cache. Named NPC follows the player for a short time.

### THE COUP AND THE 13 KINGS

---

#### 4. Karim's Confession

**Where:** Small house on the city edge where General Karim spent his last 17 years.

**Setup:** Karim was never arrested, never executed. He was moved to a house and forgotten. The house is still there — overgrown, rotting, intact.

**The Chain:** Player finds the house → locked desk → small notebook. Karim wrote every day from 1954 to 1971.

**The Reveal:** The notebook is not a memoir. It is a **list**. Every officer arrested, tortured, or executed by the council — with Karim's notes on each. He wrote them because he wanted someone to remember. Final line: *"I helped them take the nation. I could not stop what they did with it. But I will not let them erase the people they killed."*

**Reward:** One of the few documents proving what the council did. The Mazar Republic pays heavily. The Junta Remnants pay more — to destroy it.

---

#### 5. The Fourteenth Chair

**Where:** Old council chamber, Government Palace.

**Setup:** The council table still has fourteen chairs. Thirteen are modern. One is old — the original from 1952. It has never been replaced.

**The Chain:** Player enters the chamber → old chair in a corner, dusty, unused → examining the underside reveals a carving.

**The Reveal:** The carving reads *Karim*, and the date *1954*. One of the other council members carved it the day Karim was moved out. Not a memorial. A warning to whoever sat in the chair next.

**Reward:** Nothing material. But the chamber itself is a defensible safehouse — structurally sound, single entrance.

---

#### 6. The Thirteen Portraits

**Where:** Hidden gallery in the Government Palace basement.

**Setup:** Thirteen portraits on the wall. Each a different man. None labeled. All painted in the same style, the same size, by the same artist.

**The Chain:** Player photographs the portraits → shows them to survivors → one old woman recognizes two. She tells the player their names, domains, crimes. She was a servant in the palace in the 1960s.

**The Reveal:** The portraits were painted by an artist executed in 1971 — the same year Karim died. He was told to paint "the great leaders of the nation." He painted them as he saw them: grim, tired, afraid. He hid the meaning in the eyes — each subject has the same expression: a man who has done something he cannot undo.

**Reward:** The old woman becomes a lore source. If kept safe, she reveals more palace secrets.

### THE MODERN COUNCIL AND SASO

---

#### 7. Saso's File

**Where:** Intelligence service HQ, Downtown.

**Setup:** Most archives burned during the outbreak. But Saso's personal file survived — fireproof safe, collapsed room around it.

**The Chain:** Player finds the safe → needs a key (in Saso's personal quarters) → opens it → twelve pages.

**The Reveal:** Saso was born in 1971 — the year Karim died. His father, an intelligence officer, was executed by the council in 1985. Saso joined the service in 1990 to find out who killed him. By 2012, he had killed every man responsible. By 2015, he had taken the council's power. Final entry, dated 2025: *"They think I serve them. They think I am one of them. I am neither. I am what they made."*

**Reward:** Changes how the player sees Saso — not a villain, but a man destroyed and rebuilt. Reveals location of his private bunker.

---

#### 8. The Border Enemy's Liaison

**Where:** Rural safehouse in the Forest, near the northern border.

**Setup:** Someone lived here before the outbreak. Not Mazarani — a foreigner. Foreign cigarettes, foreign newspaper, a typewriter with a non-Mazarani keyboard.

**The Chain:** Player finds a locked filing cabinet → a notebook in a foreign language → a survivor translates: it's the Border Enemy liaison's personal journal.

**The Reveal:** The liaison was a translator — a young man assigned to communicate between Saso and the Border Enemy. He hated the work. He hated Saso. He wrote he would defect if he could, but there was nowhere to go. Last entry, outbreak day: *"It has begun. I am going north. I do not expect to make it. But if someone finds this — the truth is in the fort. Everything you need is in the fort."*

**Reward:** The notebook is a map to the Fort Sarran laboratory. Without it, the player may not know where to look.

---

#### 9. The Council's Last Meeting

**Where:** Council chamber, three weeks after the outbreak.

**Setup:** The council met one final time. They argued, voted, dissolved. The minutes are the last official record of the regime.

**The Chain:** Player finds the chamber → table still set → a single sheet of paper in longhand, written by the council secretary.

**The Reveal:** The minutes record the vote to dissolve the council. Seven voted to fight. Five voted to flee. One abstained. The abstention was Saso's. After the vote, he stood, said one sentence — *"I will not be remembered for this"* — and walked out. He shot the secretary on his way to the door. Her name was Lina.

**Reward:** Lina's body is still in the hallway. In her pocket: a key to a room in the Government Palace nobody knew existed.

### THE OUTBREAK AND AFTER

---

#### 10. The Water Tower

**Where:** Water Tower, Industrial Park.

**Setup:** The city's main reservoir. One of the first places the liquid concentrated. Sealed since 2025.

**The Chain:** Player climbs the tower → finds a maintenance worker's journal → the worker documented the water contamination four days before anyone else noticed. He wrote to the council. He wrote to the press. He wrote to his wife. Nobody replied.

**The Reveal:** Final entry, five days after the outbreak: he sealed himself in the tower to avoid infection. He survived three weeks. Then he ran out of food. His body is at the bottom, along with a full set of uncontaminated water purification equipment.

**Reward:** The purification equipment is one of the most valuable items in the game — it lets the player build a clean-water source at their base.

---

#### 11. The Convoy

**Where:** Military Zone, road to Fort Sarran.

**Setup:** A military convoy crashed trying to escape the fort. Twelve trucks, thirty soldiers, four civilians. None survived.

**The Chain:** Player searches the convoy → a soldier's diary → describes the last days at Fort Sarran: the experiment, the leak, the panic.

**The Reveal:** The soldier was one of the lab guards. He didn't know what the experiment was until the day it broke. Last entry: walking into the lab and seeing what was inside — then walking out, getting in his truck, driving south. *"I do not know what they made. But I know what it does. And I know it is coming south."*

**Reward:** Confirms the origin of the outbreak. Names the four civilians — one was the only survivor of the Fort Sarran staff. Their body is not in the convoy. They made it out.

---

#### 12. The Immune Child

**Where:** Suburbia house, randomly chosen from the map.

**Setup:** A family hid in their house during the outbreak. The parents were infected. The child was not.

**The Chain:** Player finds the house → parents dead in the living room → child alive upstairs, hiding in a closet.

**The Reveal:** The child is immune. Not "resistant" — genuinely immune. Their blood can develop a vaccine. Every faction wants the child.

**Reward:** Player can protect the child, deliver them to the Mazar Republic, deliver to the Junta, or abandon them. The child becomes a companion NPC if protected.

---

#### 13. The Radio Operator

**Where:** Broadcast Tower, Downtown.

**Setup:** The tower's last operator is still alive. Broadcasting on a loop for months. He hasn't left because he believes someone is coming to relieve him.

**The Chain:** Player finds the tower → operator is emaciated, sick, lucid → asks the player to take over the broadcast for one shift so he can sleep.

**The Reveal:** While broadcasting, the player hears a message on a frequency the operator didn't know about: a distress call from the Mazar Republic, addressed to the operator by name. Three months old. The relief convoy never came. He's the last of his unit.

**Reward:** The operator becomes a valuable NPC — he can broadcast messages that affect the world (calling for help, warning survivors, jamming the Junta's signal). He knows every emergency shelter in the city.

---

#### 14. The Last Photograph

**Where:** Stadium, Downtown.

**Setup:** A photographer documented the outbreak from the stadium's upper levels. His camera is still there, along with 40 rolls of film.

**The Chain:** Player finds the camera → develops film at a photography lab in the Commercial Strip → the photos reveal the outbreak's spread, day by day.

**The Reveal:** One photo, taken on Day 6, shows a figure in military uniform walking away from Fort Sarran on foot, carrying a briefcase. Photographer's notes: *"I don't know who this is. But he's the only one who walked out."*

**Reward:** Evidence. The photographer was a Mazar Republic asset. Bring the photos to the Republic for a major reputation boost and access to restricted information.

---

#### 15. The Boy in the Well

**Where:** Rural farmhouse in Farmland.

**Setup:** A farmhouse with a well. A boy, 11 years old, hiding at the bottom. Two weeks. He saw his family killed. He climbed down and hasn't come up.

**The Chain:** Player hears crying → climbs down the well → boy is terrified, dehydrated, alive.

**The Reveal:** His family was not killed by zombies. They were killed by **soldiers** — a Junta Remnant patrol sweeping the farmland for supplies. The boy saw their faces. He can identify them.

**Reward:** The boy becomes a companion NPC. He knows the location of the Junta patrol's base. Raid the base → revenge + his loyalty.

---

#### 16. The Empty Grave

**Where:** Small cemetery on the edge of Suburbia.

**Setup:** A grave, freshly dug. Blank headstone. Someone dug it recently — after the outbreak started.

**The Chain:** Player digs up the grave → sealed metal box → a single item: the crown of the House of Mazar.

**The Reveal:** The crown was thought lost in 1952. Someone — a descendant, a loyalist, or a thief — kept it hidden for 73 years. When the outbreak came, they buried it hoping someone would find it after the chaos ended.

**Reward:** Worth an enormous amount — to collectors, to the Mazar Republic (symbolically), to the Junta (as a threat), to the Border Enemy (as a pawn). The most valuable single item in the game. Keeping it marks the player as a target.

---

#### 17. The Last Voicemail

**Where:** Any house with an answering machine.

**Setup:** A random house, a landline, a blinking light. Power is out everywhere, but this house has a backup generator running.

**The Chain:** Player presses play → forty messages, each more desperate than the last → final message, Day 8, a woman to her husband saying she's leaving the city with the children and she'll call again when she reaches the border.

**The Reveal:** She never called again. The husband installed the generator. He waited by the phone until he died.

**Reward:** Nothing material. But it makes the world feel real.

---

#### 18. The Border Signal

**Where:** Any radio, tuned to 88.7 FM.

**Setup:** A frequency broadcasting a repeating string of numbers. Anyone who's listened long enough realizes it's a code.

**The Chain:** Player finds a military codebook in the Intelligence HQ → decodes the message.

**The Reveal:** The message is from the Border Enemy to Saso, sent the day before the outbreak: *"The project is terminated. We are withdrawing support. Do not contact us again."* Saso received it. He ignored it. He knew what was coming. He let it happen anyway.

**Reward:** Confirms Saso knew about the leak in advance and allowed it.

---

#### 19. The Two Hundred

**Where:** Hidden bunker in the Forest.

**Setup:** A bunker built during the Cold War, expanded by the 13 Kings, updated by Saso. Officially never existed. Capacity: 200 people, 5 years.

**The Chain:** Player finds the bunker → never used → 200 bunks, 5 years of supplies, untouched.

**The Reveal:** Built for the council, their families, and closest allies. They planned to retreat there if the regime ever fell. They never made it. The keys were distributed among the 13 Kings — and none trusted the others enough to actually use it.

**Reward:** Best base location in the game. Defendable, water, supplies, room for a large group. But far from the city — reaching it means crossing the entire map.

---

#### 20. The Diary of Saso

**Where:** Saso's private bunker, north of Fort Sarran.

**Setup:** Saso had a private bunker, separate from the council's. He kept personal effects there. He was last seen heading north — but he never made it to the bunker.

**The Chain:** Player finds the bunker → a single room with a desk and chair → on the desk, a leather-bound diary.

**The Reveal:** The diary covers 1990 to 2025. Not a confession. Not a justification. The record of a man who spent 35 years becoming the thing he hated, knew it, and did it anyway.

Final entry, dated the day of the outbreak:

> *"I have spent my life destroying the men who killed my father. I have succeeded. I have become them. There is no one left to kill. There is no one left to save. I am going north because I do not know what else to do. If someone is reading this, I want them to know: I am sorry. Not for what I did — for what I became. There is a difference. I do not know if it matters."*

**Reward:** The final piece of lore. Doesn't change the world. Changes how the player understands everything that came before.
